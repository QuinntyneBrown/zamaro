Content & tone

Zamaro talks like a worship coordinator who has booked a lot of Sundays: warm, plain and practical. The gig poster lives in the type and a handful of words, not in every sentence.

Overview

The people reading are worship coordinators, pastors and volunteers, often on a phone, often booking for a service that matters to them. They want to know who is free, what it costs and what happens next. The voice has three traits:

TraitMeansSounds likeNot like
WarmSpeaks to one church about its event, by name and date.“Seven artists are free that Saturday and will drive to Burlington.”“7 results found.”
PlainShort words, one idea per sentence, numbers as numerals.“Nothing is charged until Abigail accepts.”“No payment will be processed prior to artist confirmation.”
Pastoral but practicalRespects worship without preaching; always gives the next step.“Book Miriam and your church can be the first to say how it went.”“Bless your congregation with an anointed worship experience!”

The general writing rules (sentence length, inclusive language, spelling) are in the content foundation. This page is the product voice and the copy itself. It applies to every component, from the button to the empty state and the toast.

When to use

Gig-poster vocabulary

A few poster words give Zamaro its character. Use at most one per block, only where it fits, and never where the church needs exact meaning: money, dates, errors, status, policy.

Plain words win when

  • The church is about to pay, cancel or commit: “Pay $162.50 deposit”, “Cancel booking”.
  • Something went wrong: “We lost the signal” is the furthest the metaphor goes, and the next sentence is literal.
  • A booking changes state: Requested, Accepted, Booked, Played, Declined, Cancelled.
  • The words are about faith itself. Name the gathering as the church does (“Sunday service”, “worship night”, “funeral or memorial”) and don’t dress it up.

Structure

Most Zamaro blocks have the same four lines of copy: a kicker that sets the scene, a headline that says the point, one sentence of detail, and an action that starts with a verb.

  1. KickerWho or where, in a few words. Overline style; the dot (·) separates facts: “Sat 14 Nov · 7 free · within 120 km”.
  2. HeadlineThe point, as the reader would say it. Written in sentence case; CSS sets it in capitals.
  3. DetailOne sentence, two at most. Names the date, the place or the person.
  4. ActionA verb first, four words or fewer before any dot: “Show the lineup”, “Request to book · Sat 14 Nov”.

The error formula

What happened + what is safe + what to do. One or two sentences and a button.

We lost the signal

We couldn’t load who’s free on Saturday 14 November 2026. Your date, location and filters are kept.

Enter your church’s name so Abigail knows who’s asking.
A region error: what happened, what is kept, Try again. A field error: what is missing and why it matters.

Responsive behaviour

  • Write for 360px first. Button labels fit on one line at 360px; “Request to book · Sat 14 Nov” is the longest allowed.
  • Never truncate names with an ellipsis. “Grace Tabernacle Mass Choir” and “Daniel & Ruth Okonkwo” wrap; poster names may break anywhere rather than overflow.
  • Keep date groups together. The poster wraps between “Sat” and “14 Nov”, never inside “14 Nov”.
  • Shorten by dropping detail, not words: on phones Saved shows the heart and the count; its name stays “Saved artists, 3”.
  • Copy length does not change with the screen. If a sentence is too long for a phone, it is too long.

Accessibility

Role and pattern

Content is the accessible name of most controls, so the rules here are accessibility rules: link text that makes sense alone (WCAG 2.4.4), visible labels that start the accessible name (2.5.3), error text that says how to fix it (3.3.3) and a page language (3.1.1).

Keyboard

Not applicable to copy itself. Keyboard hints, when needed, name the key: “Press Esc to close”.

Focus

Write the first words of a link or button so they make sense when focus lands on it: “Pick Fri 20 Nov”, not “Pick”.

Labelling

  • Sentence case in the source. Screen readers may spell out text typed in capitals, and copied text keeps its case. CSS adds the poster capitals.
  • Name the object: “Save Elijah Park to your saved artists”, “Share Abigail Mensah’s profile”, “Account menu for Naomi Fraser”.
  • Mark dates up with <time datetime="2026-11-14">Sat 14 Nov</time>.
  • Symbols need words: ★ 4.9 is named “Rated 4.9 out of 5”; the marquee’s ✦ is hidden; the typed ✓ became an icon with text.
  • Other languages: Twi and Spanish song titles carry lang="tw" or lang="es" so they are pronounced properly.

Motion

Copy never moves on its own. The marquee is a static strip; nothing scrolls text automatically.

Touch

Short labels keep buttons at one line and full height. Link text in a sentence is at least two words, so it is easy to hit.

Rules of thumb

  • Canadian spelling: colour, centre, cheque, travelled, with -ize endings (organization).
  • “Email” with no hyphen, as noun and verb, following the strings in the specs (“Check your email…”, “Email or password is incorrect.”).
  • Use first names after the first mention: “Abigail Mensah” on the ticket, “Abigail” in the stub.
  • “Church” for the booker, “artist” for the musician, “service” or “event” for the gathering. Not “client”, “talent” or “gig”.
  • No exclamation marks, no emoji, no “Oops”.
